PCB Placement and Routing

PCB layout turns the schematic's logical connections into physical current paths. Placement determines whether those paths can be short, continuous, cool enough, accessible, and mechanically useful. Routing should therefore follow deliberate placement rather than repair an arbitrary arrangement of footprints.

Start exploratory placement after the first coherent schematic blocks are built; do not wait for the entire schematic to accumulate into a large pile of unplaced parts. Work back and forth among schematic design, placement, and early routing. The board will often reveal packaging, connector, pin-allocation, and support-circuit changes. Keep the editors synchronized, and complete a schematic review before final routing and release.

Establish Physical and Manufacturing Constraints

Before detailed placement, define:

  • board outline and height limits;
  • mounting holes, fasteners, standoffs, washers, and tool clearance;
  • enclosure walls, panel openings, and mechanical keepouts;
  • connector location, mating direction, cable bend radius, and insertion/removal access;
  • buttons, indicators, sensors, antennas, and adjustment points that require exposure;
  • expected assembly and rework methods;
  • manufacturer capabilities and clearances for the selected service;
  • layer arrangement, copper weight when relevant, and design rules.

Print a 1:1 view when practical and compare it with real connectors, fasteners, and unusually large components. A correct footprint can still be unusable if a cable cannot be inserted or a screwdriver cannot reach a mounting screw.

Choose a Layer Arrangement

The standard course recommendation is a four-layer, 1.6 mm board with top-side assembly. A common starting arrangement is signal and components on the outer layers with an internal ground plane and an internal power or secondary routing layer. This is a recommendation, not a universal stackup.

A two-layer board can reduce cost and may be reasonable for a simple design, but it usually requires more deliberate routing to preserve useful ground return paths. Students may choose reasonable settings for their project; course designs are limited to no more than four layers. Obtain the actual stackup and design rules from the selected manufacturer before final routing.

Do not add internal copper layers to the manufacturing output unless the design actually uses them. The exported layer set must match the chosen stackup.

Verify Footprints in the Board Context

For critical or unfamiliar parts, compare the placed footprint with the datasheet land pattern and package drawing. Check pad numbering, pitch, body outline, courtyard, pin one, exposed pads, holes, slots, and connector overhang.

Do not treat Fusion's 3D rendering as the definitive footprint check. A plausible 3D body can be offset, incorrectly modeled, or associated with pads that do not match the real part. Within Fusion, the 2D board view is the best source for pad geometry, drill locations, outlines, and copper relationships. The final Gerber and drill outputs are the ultimate proof of what the manufacturer will fabricate, so inspect them before release.

An exposed pad is not automatically ground. Its electrical connection, solder-paste pattern, thermal vias, and permitted via treatment come from the exact part and assembly process. Ordinary untreated via-in-pad can wick solder away from a joint; use it only when the land pattern and manufacturing process support it.

Place by Constraint and Current Path

Place components in this order, revisiting earlier decisions when needed:

  1. Place mechanically constrained parts: connectors, mounting holes, buttons, sensors, displays, antennas, and board-edge features.
  2. Place power entry, protection, regulators, and high-current loads so the main power path is direct.
  3. Place the microcontroller and other central devices with room for their required local support components.
  4. Place bypass capacitors, regulator components, oscillator parts, feedback networks, gate-drive loops, and other parts whose physical connection is electrically critical.
  5. Place remaining functional groups so their internal connections are compact and their interfaces face the appropriate neighboring blocks.
  6. Reserve readable silkscreen space for reference designators and functional annotations while adjusting component spacing and orientation.
  7. Reserve access for test points, programming cables, probes, and likely rework.

Avoid clustering components merely because they belong to the same schematic sheet. The physical relationship that matters is the current path and return path.

Plan the Functional Silkscreen

Silkscreen needs physical space, so plan it during component placement rather than trying to fit every label after routing. Each populated part should have a legible reference designator that remains reasonably associated with that part after assembly. Also reserve space for the functional annotations selected in PCB Test Access and Rework.

The standard Fusion/EAGLE convention is to print reference designators from the Names layer and not print component values from the Values layer. Values often become cluttered, may change without a footprint change, and are better recovered from the schematic and bill of materials. Show a value only when it provides a specific assembly or use benefit.

This guide owns the physical space and layer decisions for silkscreen; the test-access guide owns the label content. Do not duplicate the same label in multiple forms merely because space is available.

Identify Critical Current and Return Paths

Current flows in a loop. A signal trace carries outgoing current, and copper elsewhere carries its return. At low frequencies the return may spread through available conductors; as edge rates increase, the return tends to follow near the signal path through the adjacent reference plane. Gaps, slots, or accidental plane splits can force a long detour and increase loop area, noise, and coupling.

Mark the paths that deserve attention before routing:

  • source through regulator and back to source;
  • local bypass loop from capacitor to device supply and ground pins;
  • switched-current loops in motor drivers, converters, and other power stages;
  • clocks and other fast-edge signals;
  • sensitive analog inputs and references;
  • differential interfaces and their termination where applicable;
  • high-current output and return paths.

Keep a continuous reference under important signals. Group noisy and sensitive circuits through placement and return-path control; do not split ground automatically into “analog” and “digital” regions without a device-specific reason and a deliberate crossing strategy.

Place Cbypass so the supply-pin-to-capacitor-to-ground loop is short and direct. A schematic that draws all capacitors in one corner may still be electrically correct, but the PCB must place each local capacitor at the pin or supply group it serves. Do not move a bypass capacitor away from the pin merely to make probing easier; add a separate accessible test point.

Route in Priority Order

  1. Route critical power and switched-current loops using widths, copper areas, and vias appropriate to current and temperature rise.
  2. Route clocks, sensitive analog nets, differential pairs, and other constrained signals according to their device requirements.
  3. Route remaining signals while preserving continuous return paths.
  4. Add and review planes or pours. Confirm that narrow necks, isolated islands, thermal reliefs, and antipads do not break intended connections.
  5. Add stitching vias where they serve a clear return-path, shielding, thermal, or structural purpose.

Set trace width, clearance, via size, and copper-to-edge distance from electrical needs and the selected manufacturer's rules. A 6 mil signal trace is a common manufacturable starting point for ordinary low-current signals, but use wider traces when space permits and size power paths for their current and temperature rise. Ninety-degree corners are usually less important on these boards than continuity, clearance, manufacturability, current capacity, and return paths.

Review the Layout

Before release:

  • Board outline, holes, cutouts, and keepouts match the mechanical design.
  • Connectors, controls, indicators, and test points remain accessible in the assembled system.
  • Footprints, pad numbers, pin one, polarity, and exposed-pad treatment match the exact parts.
  • Critical current loops and return paths are short and intentional.
  • Power widths and copper areas are supported by current and thermal estimates.
  • Important signals do not cross reference-plane gaps without a planned return path.
  • No required connection remains unrouted after pours are regenerated.
  • Copper islands and narrow plane necks have been reviewed.
  • Silkscreen does not obscure pads and remains readable after assembly.
  • The design rule check (DRC) findings are fixed or documented as intentional exceptions.
  • Schematic and PCB are synchronized.
  • A 1:1 print or mechanical model has been checked where it adds useful evidence.

DRC verifies declared geometric rules, not physical fit, current capacity, signal integrity, assembly orientation, or functionality.

Result of This Stage

The result is a routed board whose electrical paths, physical interfaces, and manufacturing assumptions can be explained. Continue planning test access and rework throughout layout, then use the release checklist on a specific revision.
