PCB First Power Up and Bring Up

Bring-up is the controlled process of learning what an assembled board actually does. Begin with low-risk observations, compare them with explicit expectations, and stop when the evidence no longer supports the next step.

Do not connect every load and apply unrestricted power to see what happens. Plan the sequence from the schematic, release archive, and test-access features.

Prepare the Plan and Tools

Have the following available as appropriate:

  • current-limited bench power supply;
  • digital multimeter;
  • oscilloscope and suitable probes;
  • identified programmer/debugger and cable;
  • logic analyzer or USB-UART connection;
  • non-contact temperature measurement or thermal camera when available;
  • known-good cables, adapters, and loads;
  • schematic, PCB layout, BOM/assembly record, and MCU documentation.

Before powering the board, record:

Item Planned value or action
Input connection and polarity
Nominal input voltage and allowed range
Expected startup current
Expected steady-state current
Initial current limit and rationale
Loads or subsystems disconnected initially
First rail and test point to measure
Stop conditions

For a small MCU and logic board with high-power loads disconnected, 50–100 mA is often a useful first current-limit range. Use it only when the estimated startup and steady-state current fit within that range. Increase the initial limit for a circuit that is expected to need more, but still choose the lowest value that allows the intentionally powered section to start. Capacitor charging can produce a brief transient; sustained unexpected current is different evidence.

Inspect the Unpowered Board

Compare the physical board with the released design and assembly record.

  • Board revision is the intended revision.
  • Vendor-assembled, group-assembled, and DNP parts match the plan.
  • ICs, diodes, LEDs, polarized capacitors, connectors, and other polarized parts have the expected orientation.
  • No solder bridges, solder balls, debris, damaged pads, lifted traces, cracked parts, or incomplete joints are visible.
  • Fine-pitch devices, power components, and connectors have received close inspection.

With power disconnected, check for unintended shorts between input and ground, between power rails, and across critical connector pins. Resistance from a rail to ground is circuit-dependent and may change as capacitors charge from the meter. A nonzero reading is not proof that the board is safe, and a low reading is not automatically a short. Compare with the schematic and, when possible, a justified expectation or known-good board.

If the evidence suggests an unintended short, do not apply power. Isolate sections and inspect until the reading is explained.

Apply Controlled Power

Disconnect high-power loads or optional subsystems when the design permits. Set the correct input voltage and planned current limit, connect with verified polarity, and arrange the first measurement before enabling the supply.

Enable power while observing supply voltage and current.

Stop immediately if:

  • current remains at the limit without an expected explanation;
  • the input or a rail collapses unexpectedly;
  • smoke, odor, sound, visible arcing, or rapid temperature rise occurs;
  • a component exceeds its expected temperature or rating;
  • any planned stop condition is reached.

Do not increase the current limit merely to make an unexplained limiting condition disappear. Power off, discharge the board as required, and investigate.

Verify Power Before the Controller

Measure the input and every energized rail at the planned test points. Compare the values with expected ranges and check startup or ripple where relevant.

Use a suitable instrument to inspect temperature; do not touch an unknown hot component. A thermal camera, infrared thermometer with understood limitations, thermocouple, or other appropriate method can provide safer evidence.

If the rails are correct and current is plausible, check basic reset and enable states. Indicators can provide quick clues, but a dark power LED does not prove that the rail is absent, and a lit LED does not prove that every rail is correct.

Connect the Debugger and Minimal Firmware

Connect the verified programming/debug tool. For the course ST-Link modules, confirm the six-pin SWD orientation, common ground, target-voltage reference, and cable pinout before connection.

If the debugger cannot identify the controller, measure rather than guess:

  1. Confirm controller supply pins at the device.
  2. Confirm ground continuity and target-voltage sensing.
  3. Observe reset and boot states.
  4. Check SWDIO and SWCLK continuity and for shorts or swapped pins.
  5. Apply the device-specific recovery procedure if justified.

Use minimal firmware that produces a clear observation without energizing every subsystem. Examples include a controlled status LED, a periodic UART message, or a timer output. Choose the test that fits the design.

Do not probe a crystal node directly unless the selected device guidance and measurement setup account for probe loading. Prefer debugger evidence, a buffered clock output, or a peripheral output derived from the system clock.

Validate One Subsystem at a Time

After power and controller operation are established, enable one functional block at a time. Record the expected observation before the test. Begin with lower-energy interfaces and proceed toward high-current loads only after control signals, protections, and safe states are verified.

A useful order is:

  1. GPIO and indicators;
  2. UART or another simple debug channel;
  3. I²C, SPI, or other digital peripherals;
  4. analog references, inputs, and sensors;
  5. low-energy outputs;
  6. drivers, actuators, heaters, motors, or other high-power loads.

Power off before rework or changing components unless the procedure is explicitly designed for live adjustment and the hazards are controlled.

Change one thing at a time when possible. Record component substitutions, solder changes, jumper states, firmware revision, and supply settings so the result can be reproduced.

Use Measurements to Narrow the Problem

Observation Possible causes Next discriminating measurement
Supply is in current limit Short, reversed part, excessive load, wrong limit, startup transient Power off; isolate rails or subsystems and compare resistance/current by section
Little or no current Open input path, switch/jumper state, missing part, wrong connector Measure voltage from the connector through each power-path stage
Regulator output is wrong Wrong input, incorrect support parts, overload, thermal shutdown, assembly fault Measure regulator input, enable, output, load current, and temperature
MCU not detected Supply, ground, target-voltage sense, reset, SWD wiring, boot/recovery state Measure device supply and reset, then check SWD continuity and cable orientation
Communication fails No common ground, level mismatch, swapped direction, missing/incorrect bias, firmware configuration Observe both ends of the interface and compare idle levels and transitions
Intermittent behavior Marginal power, poor joint, floating input, timing or firmware issue Correlate rails, reset, and relevant signals with the failure event

These observations do not identify one certain cause. Use the next measurement to remove alternatives.

Completion Evidence

Bring-up is complete for the intended milestone when the record shows which rails, controller functions, interfaces, and loads were tested; what was measured; what remains unresolved; and which hardware and firmware revisions produced the result.
